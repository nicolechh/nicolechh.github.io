Raspberry AI: masking on the canvas, not in a modal

Multi-material masking, redesigned as a mode that leaves the rest of the workspace usable.

Role
Product Designer
Timeline
Apr 7–20, 2026
Agency
Interlude Studio

Summary

Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur adipiscing elit.

Raspberry AI turns fashion sketches into photorealistic renders for brands like Steve Madden and Levi's. Boards, its canvas workspace, lets designers compare options side by side while images generate.

Sketch to Render had shipped in Boards but multi-material masking hadn't. Applying fabric to part of a garment still meant leaving Boards for the classic app's modal. I designed masking as a canvas mode instead of a window, so the rest of a designer's options stay reachable. Shipped and still live.

  • Role: Sole designer on multi-material masking in Boards
  • Team: Design reviews with Sabrina Wen and Robyn Dang at Interlude Studio, and Raspberry's team of designers, PMs, and engineers.
  • Timeline: 2 weeks design
  • Scope: Multi-material masking for Boards (masking mode, toolbar, and states)
  • Platform: Web app
  • Status: Shipped; live as of writing

The Problem

Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur adipiscing elit.

Boards was under development, and Raspberry had scoped the build. Every tool in the classic app needed a canvas equivalent. Multi-material masking in Sketch to Render was still missing.

Masking is where a designer makes decisions worth comparing. This fabric on the collar, that one on the sleeve, up to three at once. In the classic app it happened in a modal: a window that takes over the screen, does its job, and hands you back.

A modal would have worked anywhere else but Boards exists so a designer can compare options and reference existing designs. Masking is exactly when they'd want to check and a modal on top puts the board out of reach.

Constraints

  • A toolset built for a modal. Masking already had three selection tools (smart mask, lasso, brush) each with its own controls, laid out for a modal.
  • Two navigation systems on the same gestures. The masking tool and the canvas each have their own pan and zoom.
  • Platform rules I couldn't change. Up to three materials, each masked separately, and nothing to generate without a masked area for each.
  • Boards was still being built while I designed against it. The surface I was adding to was still changing.

The Decisions

1 — Mask in place, not in a modal

Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur adipiscing elit.

Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur adipiscing elit.

Porting the modal on top of the canvas was the safe option: proven, focused, with room for every control.

I made masking a mode instead. Uploading a material image and entering masking mode focuses the image and raises a toolbar carrying both selection tools and materials. The canvas stays visible behind it.

The reason is the same one that justifies creating Boards: masking is where a designer decides what differentiates one option from others on the canvas. A modal preserves the work underneath, but it makes every check deliberate (close it, look, reopen) and what costs a click gets consulted less.

Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur adipiscing elit.

Moving onto the canvas traded space for navigation. A modal has room for every control, a floating toolbar doesn't, so selection tools nest in a dropdown and switching costs an extra click. The canvas already pans and zooms so masking's navigation came off the toolbar entirely, combining two systems into one, a simplification the modal couldn't make.

2 — Let designers leave mid-mask

Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur adipiscing elit.

Using the canvas's navigation raised a new question: does it stay live while masking?

Locking it would have been safer, but a frozen canvas recreates the modal's problem in a different shape — the board visible, still unreachable. A designer masking a collar might want to compare with a previous version or another colourway. There's little point moving masking onto the canvas if it stops working the moment you use it.

The cost is now a designer can navigate away from the image mid-edit. The toolbar anchors to the image rather than the viewport, tracking its position, holding its size as the canvas zooms, and if someone leaves it behind entirely, a snackbar offers to take them back.

Craft & System

The node does three jobs

  • It holds up to three materials, mirrored from the toolbar, visible even when masking is closed, tracking state and letting a click reopen masking directly.
  • It also gates generation, disabled until every material is masked, with a running count (e.g. 1/3 materials masked) showing what's outstanding.

Anchored to the image

Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur adipiscing elit.

The toolbar anchors to the image, following pans and holding size through zooms, so it's never a four-pixel strip or oversized slab.

Five controls, two exits

Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur adipiscing elit.

Undo, redo, save, reset, and close. Two exits: save then leave, or leave without saving.

Interrupted, not cancelled

Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur adipiscing elit.

Panning away doesn't end masking. A snackbar keeps the mask open, offering a route back or an exit.

Accessibility

The snackbar has no timer. It's a status indicator, not a notification, so it stays until resolved. A self-dismissing one fails whoever takes longest to reach it.

Ship & Collaboration

Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur adipiscing elit.

The behaviours that mattered here were better demonstrated than described: a toolbar that follows an image's position but not its scale, a snackbar that fires when a pan carries you off the image.

I prototyped masking mode with Claude Code and walked through it live with the Raspberry team. Questions got answered by doing rather than describing. It shipped mostly as designed, the only change being a text reset button instead of an icon.

The Outcome

Masking data was requested but unavailable. There's no mask event and no mask flag on generation. The only trace is a generic click on the toolbar labels, which misses anyone who masks by brushing and confirming. That's a floor, not a count.

The measures I'd want are whether designers actually moved to masking in Boards, and how often a mask session ends with panning away and returning through the snackbar — the one decision here that predicted a behaviour, and so the one a number could actually settle.

Reflection

Nesting tools in a dropdown cost a click that used to be free: switching selection tools mid-mask. I took that trade to keep the toolbar small enough for the canvas, but I'd revisit it. Surfacing two or three most-used tools would save a click on nearly every mask. Which two or three is a question about usage, and that data was unavailable to me.
